ALEX OLIVER — INTERVIEW FLOW
November 19, 2026 | 50-minute conversation + 20-minute audience Q&A

Direction: his experience → what to build → how to scale → where to draw limits.
Ask the main question. Use one follow-up if useful. Leave room for stories.

INTRO | 0–4 min

“Alex Oliver is a partner at Andreessen Horowitz in American Dynamism,
the firm's practice backing companies in areas like defense, aerospace
and manufacturing. He helps aerospace and national-security founders
bring their technology into use. Before a16z, he served as an Army infantry
and intelligence officer, led maritime autonomy at the Defense Innovation
Unit, and advised Special Operations Command on technology.

Alex, thanks for being here. Would you give us a short introduction in your
own words—and tell us what drew you from the military into working with founders?”

Leave his venture-firm fellowship out; let him tell that turning point.
Title: partner, go-to-market. Don't introduce him as a GP.

1. EXPERIENCE → JUDGMENT | 4–10 min
Niche: military users, intelligence, prototype adoption.

What did your deployments and government work teach you that you would
have missed if you'd gone straight into venture capital?

Concrete follow-up: At DIU, you worked on amphibious mine-clearing robots.
What did operators need that the technology teams initially misunderstood?

Know: DIU named him on A2RV in 2022; two teams selected from 67 submissions.
Later Marine Corps orders establish procurement, not his role in every award.

Bridge: “How does that change which problems you encourage founders to tackle?”

2. WHY BUILD IN DEFENSE? | 10–17 min
Niche: overlooked advantages, real demand, political risk.

For a student choosing between defense and enterprise software, what is
an advantage of defense that outsiders underestimate?

Follow-up: Who would you advise against building here?

How does a founder build around a need that survives elections?
After this month's election, what do you expect to change for defense
startups—and through which budgets or procurement decisions?

Listen for: user versus buyer; durable mission versus temporary priority.
Treat election effects as his forecast, not an established outcome.

Bridge: “Even with demand, someone has to build the capacity to deliver.”

3. PEACETIME INDUSTRY | 17–25 min — GO DEEP
Niche: readiness, commercial manufacturing, incentives.

What does the ideal defense industrial base look like during peacetime?
Which capacity must we pay to keep ready, and which should come from
commercial factories that can switch to defense production?

Challenge: How do you preserve readiness without insulating suppliers
from competition and rewarding poor performance?

Follow-up: What cannot realistically be built or converted after a war starts?

Know: commercial production and military surge capacity aren't interchangeable.
Ask about tooling, skilled labor, qualification and time—not just factory count.

Bridge: “That gets us to your argument about magazine depth.”

4. AFFORDABLE MASS | 25–33 min — GO DEEP
Niche: cost per useful effect, mission requirements, production rate.

A criticism of your magazine-depth argument is that cheaper missiles do
different jobs. Where is that objection right, and where does it miss your point?

Follow-up: Is the bigger opportunity replacing expensive systems, changing
the mix of systems, or redesigning how we produce them?

Press: What would force a successful new supplier to keep innovating once
it becomes an incumbent?

Know: his essay favors a mix. Compare mission, reliability, support costs and
replacement rate. Don't assume a cheaper weapon offers identical capability.

Bridge: “The same question applies to lessons we take from current wars.”

5. WHAT TRANSFERS FROM UKRAINE? | 33–38 min
Niche: drones, contested airspace, electronic warfare, standoff.

How much of the cheap-drone lesson from Ukraine depends on that particular
battlefield? Where would you expect it to transfer—and where would it fail?

Follow-up: Can we choose to fight at standoff, or can an adversary force
us into conditions where cheap drones matter?

Know: standoff means operating beyond the opponent's effective reach.
Don't equate remotely piloted drones with autonomous swarms, or assume
Ukraine and Iran demonstrate the same systems under comparable conditions.

Bridge: “Your case for autonomy also rests on protecting people.”

6. VALUES, LIMITS AND AI COMPETITION | 38–47 min
Niche: protection versus enhancement; human judgment; arms-race incentives.

Your essay argues that protecting soldiers gives us a moral reason to
develop autonomy. Where does that obligation stop?

Optional thought experiment: Body armor protects a soldier. Hypothetical
genetic enhancement might also improve survival. What principle separates
an acceptable protective technology from an unacceptable intervention?

If useful: How do consent, reversibility and risks to civilians change the answer?

On AI: How do we avoid a race where every country deploys systems it doesn't
trust because it fears the others will deploy first?

Follow-up: What restraint could be verified without leaving the country
that accepts it at a serious disadvantage?

Ground it: What decision would you delegate to AI today, and what would
you require a human to retain—even if that makes us slower?

Optional cyber branch: If AI makes finding vulnerabilities cheap, does the
hard problem become deploying fixes? What should government prioritize?

7. CLOSE → AUDIENCE | 47–50 min

What's one problem students here could investigate with a real user
over the next six weeks—and what would tell them it's worth pursuing?

“Let's bring in the room.” Protect 20 minutes for questions.
Final question if time: What have you changed your mind about recently?

IF TIME RUNS SHORT
Keep his story, industrial capacity, affordable mass and one AI question.
Cut the genetic-enhancement hypothetical and cyber branch first.
For any abstract answer: “Can you give us one example?”
