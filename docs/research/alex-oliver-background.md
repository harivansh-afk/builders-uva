## The concrete DIU story: mines, surf zones, and transition

### October 12, 2022: independently documented role

DIU explicitly identifies **MAJ Alex Oliver, Program Manager**, in its announcement of the Autonomous Amphibious Response Vehicle (A2RV). The mission was explosive-hazard detection, identification, inspection, and neutralization across shallow water, surf, and beach. DIU and Marine Corps Systems Command selected two approaches from 67 submissions: Greensea/Bayonet's Bayonet 250 crawler and Nauticus/VideoRay's Terranaut. Both were intended to integrate with Marine mission-command systems and use open architectures. The announcement connected them to the Marine Corps' Littoral Explosive Ordnance Neutralization (LEON) program of record and planned user evaluations by fall 2023. Oliver specifically emphasized extra funding that enabled multiple competing performers. This establishes his direct association with the program; it does not prove delivery, operational acceptance, or an individual technical invention. [DIU announcement](https://www.diu.mil/latest/diu-usmc-prototyping-two-state-of-the-art-autonomous-amphibious-response)

## Four essays: claims, tensions, and questions

These are primary evidence of Oliver's published views, not independent validation of every operational statistic or vendor-performance claim. Three are coauthored; do not imply sole authorship.

### Magazine Depth Affordability Is a Choice — September 15, 2026; Alex Oliver

His central argument is that sustained replacement capacity matters more than inventory alone, and affordable production requires designs and supply chains chosen for scale. He criticizes irregular government demand, favors commercial components and privately financed factories, and argues for shifting some spending toward cheaper entrants while retaining necessary advanced weapons. A valuable nuance is his acknowledgement that reliability changes effective cost. Treat comparisons involving Tomahawk, PrSM, Blackbeard, Barracuda, and Anthem as the author's procurement argument, not settled like-for-like performance evidence. The piece explicitly acknowledges capital from investors such as a16z. [Essay](https://a16z.com/magazine-depth-affordability-is-a-choice/)

Interview question: **“When a cheaper weapon promises more mass, what evidence tells you it produces more useful effects—not just more units? What reliability or mission failure would make you choose the expensive system?”** Follow-up: what must a founder prove before building a factory against uncertain demand?

### How to Win a Space War — June 22, 2026; Christian Keil and Alex Oliver

The essay organizes strategy around launch throughput, replenishment, manufacturing, distributed architectures, and protection of the orbital environment. Its distinctive tension: deter through credible capabilities while avoiding debris and escalation that make space unusable. It also treats ground infrastructure, spectrum rules, cybersecurity, and electronic warfare as central; the problem is larger than spacecraft hardware. Portfolio examples, including Northwood, create an investment-firm perspective worth recognizing. Do not use dramatic claims about adversary behavior, nuclear effects, or precise satellite losses without separate verification. [Essay](https://a16z.com/how-to-win-a-space-war/)

Interview question: **“You argue for strength in space without destroying the domain. What observable result would convince you an investment increased deterrence rather than merely added another target?”** Student-facing follow-up: which overlooked ground-system or software bottleneck can a small team actually address?

### “No Man Left Behind”: American Technology Ships with Our Values — May 11, 2026; Daniel Penny, Alex Oliver, Zachary Chen

The authors argue that protecting personnel is a moral reason to develop autonomy and that values become engineering requirements. They favor greater autonomy production and reject withholding protective capability as a means of preserving political restraint. This is a normative strategic argument, not proof that any particular autonomous system is safer. The text moves between remotely piloted FPV systems, AI assistance, and autonomy; ask which degree of autonomy he means. Avoid repeating its unverified operation-loss estimates as established fact. [Essay](https://a16z.com/no-man-left-behind-american-technology-ships-with-our-values/)

Interview question: **“You say values should become engineering requirements. Give us one autonomy requirement you would refuse to relax—even if it made the system slower, more expensive, or less competitive.”** Follow-up: who signs off, and what failure evidence changes the deployment decision?

### DoW Contracting for Startups 101 — April 23, 2026; Ryan McEntush, Leila Hay, Alex Oliver

The current page explicitly incorporates late-2025 reforms and early-2026 terminology. Its durable thesis is that adoption requires navigating budgets and buyers, beyond building a useful prototype. It explains budgeting timelines, appropriation categories, prototype pathways, and movement toward procurement. It distinguishes access to facilities through CRADAs from direct funding. Use this as the authors' founder-advice framework, not a substitute for current legal or acquisition guidance. [Essay](https://a16z.com/dow-contracting-for-startups-101/)

Interview question: **“A student team has a working prototype and an enthusiastic military user. What evidence would you require before telling them to hire and build around that demand?”** Follow-up: which person owns the need, which owns the money, and what changes when the original champion leaves?

## Six uniquely grounded interview angles

1. **The prototype-to-procurement handoff.** Use A2RV → Bayonet → LEON. Ask: “What had to be decided before a successful demo became something the Marines could repeatedly buy? Which handoff almost failed?” Seek a concrete decision, not a general complaint about bureaucracy. Source: DIU announcement and later procurement releases above.
2. **Keeping competition alive.** Oliver publicly thanked the funder that enabled two A2RV approaches. Ask: “When is funding two technical approaches worth more than pushing one winner faster? What evidence would make you end the second track?” Source: DIU announcement.
3. **Values as an acceptance test.** Ask for a specific requirement, accountable owner, and failure threshold. Connect his essay to a mine-clearance robot that physically moves people away from danger. Source: values essay and A2RV announcement.
4. **Affordable effects versus cheap hardware.** Ask which measured failure or logistics cost invalidates an attractive unit price, then how founders prove the distinction before scale. Source: magazine-depth essay.
5. **Founder advice that survives personnel turnover.** Present an enthusiastic-user scenario and ask for evidence of durable procurement demand. Invite a mistake he made from the government side. Source: contracting essay and present GTM remit.
6. **The neglected bottleneck students could build.** Use the space essay's ground infrastructure and non-hardware dependencies. Ask him to name a neglected problem, the first customer, and a six-week experiment that could disprove the opportunity. Source: space essay.

### Two fair challenges, framed as interview reasoning

These are proposed analytical questions, not sourced claims about a program's actual failures:

1. **Capital can advance production but cannot create a funded buyer.** If factories are built before government orders arrive, who carries the stranded-capacity risk, and what would make Oliver advise against scaling? This tests the boundary of his industrial-capacity thesis rather than assuming every factory is good investment.
2. **Saving friendly lives is not the whole deployment test.** How does he reconcile reduced operator exposure with risks to civilians, loss of communications, and unclear accountability? Ask for a concrete deployment condition or measurable threshold, not a generic discussion of whether autonomy is good or bad.
